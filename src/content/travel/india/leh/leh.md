# Leh — When the Mountains Became Real

**9–17 July 2022**

Leh was not my first visit to Ladakh. I had been there once before, in 2013, on a trip with a colleague. But that visit had happened without Pragati.

This time, she was coming with me.

Leh had been a distant dream for her. In her mind, it was almost an inaccessible place — somewhere far away in the mountains that required a great deal of effort to reach. I had a feeling that actually going there would change that perception.

It did.

## Getting to Leh

We flew from Chennai to Delhi and then onwards to Leh.

The first two days were deliberately kept for acclimatisation. At high altitude, the best thing to do is often nothing at all. We took medication, slowed down and allowed ourselves time to get used to the altitude rather than immediately trying to conquer the place.

It worked. The altitude sickness I had built up in my own mind as something to be feared never really became a problem for either of us.

Our initial plan was to stay in a hotel in Leh. That changed when Pragati's college friend, who was serving in the Army, heard that we had booked accommodation. He immediately asked us not to worry about hotels and arranged Army guesthouse accommodation according to our route.

That changed the character of the trip completely.

We stayed in Army accommodation in Leh, Partapur and Tangtse. Because we were in our late forties and were staying around Army establishments, some of the soldiers assumed we were senior officials. They would salute us whenever they saw us.

It was extremely awkward.

We were simply guests, but explaining that to every person we met would have been even more awkward, so we quietly accepted the salutes and moved on.

The food was another pleasant surprise. Meals in the Army establishments were simple, home-like and exceptionally good. After a day of travelling in the mountains, sitting down to that food was something we genuinely looked forward to.

## Khardung La and Nubra

Once we had acclimatised, our Innova became our transport for the entire circuit — Leh, Nubra, Thang, Pangong Tso and back to Leh.

Khardung La was our first major encounter with the high mountain roads.

The pass itself was far more crowded than I had expected. Bikers and tourists were everywhere, patiently taking turns to photograph themselves beside the famous Khardung La sign.

The altitude, however, did not trouble us. Two days of acclimatisation had done its job.

From there we continued towards Nubra Valley.

Hunder was one of the memorable stops. The double-humped Bactrian camels were something I had wanted to see again, and the landscape itself was striking. We also tried some of the go-karting-style rides in the valley, which added a lighter side to the journey.

The Maitreya Buddha statue in Nubra was another sight that stayed with us. Its scale and setting made it feel completely different from anything we had encountered elsewhere in the trip.

We continued towards Turtuk and then Thang, moving progressively closer to the border.

## Thang and the last post

Thang is not a place one simply drives into as a tourist. Access requires Army permission.

Our Army friend had arranged the permissions for us, including a visit towards the last post.

The experience was unique.

At one point we could see Pakistani Army posts across the landscape.

There was something almost surreal about standing there looking towards another country's military positions and realising that while we were looking at them, they were quite possibly looking back at us.

Our driver was not particularly happy about going there. He appeared to prefer taking us to another tourist location from where we could have seen a similar landscape. But we had come specifically for this experience, and we stuck to the plan.

His irritation was evident for some time afterwards, until the journey eventually took us towards Pangong.

## Pangong Tso

Pangong Tso was the highlight of the trip.

There are plenty of photographs of Pangong. They show the colours, the mountains and the enormous expanse of the lake. But photographs don't really explain what it feels like to stand there.

Its beauty is something that has to be experienced.

The landscape seemed almost unreal — the lake stretching out between the mountains, constantly changing character with the light and the sky.

After seeing it, I understood why people speak about Pangong with such fascination. Even having visited Leh before, this felt like a completely different experience because I was seeing it through Pragati's eyes as well.

## Back to Leh

We returned to Leh after completing the circuit.

With the long-distance driving finished, we rented a Royal Enfield Meteor and spent the remaining time exploring around Leh together.

We rode towards Magnetic Hill, Gurudwara Pathar Sahib and the Sangam, enjoying the freedom of moving around at our own pace.

This part of the trip was different from the circuit in the Innova. The car had taken us across the major distances, but the motorcycle gave us the feeling of actually being on the road and in the landscape.

## A different Leh for Pragati

For me, one of the most satisfying parts of this trip was seeing Pragati's perception of Leh change.

She had imagined Ladakh as an inaccessible, almost unreachable destination. After actually spending a week there, the mental barrier disappeared.

Leh was not inaccessible.

It was simply a place with a very different geography.

There were roads, hotels, tourists, bikers, markets and all the other things one associates with a tourist destination. But surrounding all of that was an extraordinary landscape that made Leh unique.

My 2013 visit had introduced me to Ladakh.

The 2022 journey made it special because I could experience it with Pragati.

And somewhere between the Army guesthouses, the mountain roads, the awkward salutes, the Royal Enfield ride and the vast blue expanse of Pangong Tso, the trip reminded me of something I have learned repeatedly through travel: sometimes a place becomes more memorable not because it is new to you, but because you get to see it for the first time through someone else's eyes.
