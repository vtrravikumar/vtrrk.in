*Some rides are about reaching a destination. Others are about enjoying every kilometre between here and there.*

There is something wonderfully unhurried about riding a Royal Enfield Classic 350.

The slow *dug… dug… dug…* of the engine, the breeze against your face, the changing scenery and the road stretching ahead—it all has a way of quietening the noise in your head. For a little while, the world becomes simpler. There is the road, the motorcycle and you.

I bought my Royal Enfield in October 2020. It has never been my office commute motorcycle. I bought it purely for leisure, for those moments when I feel like getting out, riding for a while and returning with a clearer head.

Nearly six years later, the odometer has barely crossed 3,500 kilometres.

That might sound like a poor return on a motorcycle. But perhaps we are measuring the wrong thing.

## Not every motorcycle needs to earn its keep

We live in a world that measures almost everything.

Cars are judged by mileage. Phones by screen time and features. Holidays by the number of places visited. Even hobbies can become exercises in optimisation, with targets, tracking apps and performance statistics.

Why should a motorcycle be any different?

My Classic 350 doesn't need to carry me to an office every morning. It doesn't have to fight through Chennai traffic five days a week. It doesn't need to justify itself by accumulating thousands of kilometres every year.

Sometimes, I ride because I feel like riding. Sometimes, the bike stays parked for weeks. And sometimes, just knowing that I have a motorcycle waiting for me is enough to bring a smile to my face.

The joy isn't in how often I use it. It's in the pleasure it gives me when I do.

## That unmistakable dug dug

The Classic 350 isn't about speed or aggressive acceleration. At least, that's not what draws me to it.

It's the character.

There is a particular satisfaction in starting the engine, settling onto the seat and feeling that familiar thump beneath you. The rhythm is almost meditative. You find yourself paying attention to things that would otherwise disappear into the background—the road surface, a line of trees, a turn you hadn't noticed before, the changing light as the day moves along.

And then there is the air on your face.

Even a relatively short ride can feel like a small escape. You leave behind the familiar routines, the endless notifications and the sense that you should always be doing something productive.

For a while, you simply ride.

Of course, the wind isn't always romantic, Chennai's traffic certainly doesn't cooperate with every motorcyclist's dreams, and the heat can quickly remind you that you live in Tamil Nadu. Real riding has its inconveniences.

But perhaps that is part of the appeal. You take the road as it comes, rather than expecting it to behave according to your plans.

## Riding in your 50s feels different

When you're younger, there is often a desire to do more, go farther and get there faster. There is nothing wrong with that. But as you move through life, your relationship with time can change.

At 50, I find the idea of an enjoyable ride more appealing than a race against the clock.

I don't need to prove anything by riding at a particular speed or covering a particular distance. I can enjoy a short ride without feeling that it should have been longer. I can turn back when I want to. I can stop for a cup of tea, watch the world go by and head home whenever the mood takes me.

There is a quiet freedom in that.

Riding doesn't have to be an escape from responsibilities or an attempt to recapture youth. It can simply be something you enjoy in the life you have now.

And I suspect that is one of the underrated pleasures of getting older: becoming a little more comfortable doing things purely because they make you happy.

## The 3,500-kilometre question

My motorcycle has covered barely 3,500 kilometres in almost six years. I could probably have travelled that distance in a few weeks if I'd used it regularly.

But the number doesn't tell you much about what owning the bike means to me.

It doesn't measure the anticipation of a ride, the familiar sound of the engine, the satisfaction of returning home after some time on the road or the simple pleasure of owning something that exists for no reason other than enjoyment.

I don't feel the need to invent a grand explanation for it.

I like my bike. I like riding it. That is enough.

Perhaps we sometimes forget that not everything we own, learn or do has to become an investment, a side hustle, a productivity hack or a source of measurable achievement.

Some things are allowed to be just for us.

## A month on the road—someday

There is still one idea I keep coming back to: taking the Classic 350 on a month-long ride somewhere.

I haven't done it yet. I don't have a fixed route or departure date. It's one of those plans waiting patiently in the background, the sort of thing you tell yourself you'll do someday when the time feels right.

I imagine a journey where the days aren't packed with destinations. A route chosen for the experience rather than the distance. Unhurried mornings, unfamiliar roads, local food, unexpected conversations and enough flexibility to stop wherever something catches my attention.

Maybe it will be a journey through the hills. Maybe it will take me along the coast and into places I've never explored properly. I haven't decided.

And perhaps that is part of the pleasure of the idea. It is still open, still possible, still something to look forward to.

The bike has been waiting. In a sense, so have I.

When that journey happens, I want to enjoy the preparation as much as the ride itself. There will be practical things to plan—servicing, riding gear, accommodation, route choices and daily distances. A month on the road deserves care, not just enthusiasm.

But the dream doesn't need to become a checklist today.

For now, a shorter ride is perfectly fine.

## The freedom to ride at your own pace

I sometimes wonder whether we underestimate the value of having something in our lives that doesn't need to be justified.

My Royal Enfield isn't a daily necessity. It isn't a business. It isn't a fitness programme or a travel challenge. And it certainly isn't winning any awards for annual kilometres.

It's a motorcycle I bought in October 2020 because I wanted one, and one I still enjoy owning.

At this stage of life, that feels like a perfectly good reason.

The open road will still be there. The Classic will still make that familiar thump. And one day, when the time is right, perhaps I'll point it towards somewhere distant and keep going for a whole month.

Until then, I'll take the rides I can.

A little breeze. A little *dug dug*. A little less hurry.

Sometimes, freedom doesn't need to be any more complicated than that.
